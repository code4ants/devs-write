---
title: The road I didn't dare to take
---

By the time I get home, it's late.

My birthday. Thirty-eight years old.

Three jobs behind me, a headache settling behind my eyes, and a bottle of wine waiting on the kitchen counter.

Four months of saving for one small pleasure. I should be happy.

I'd planned to read tonight, drink my wine slowly and pretend, just for a few hours, that my life belonged to me. Instead, all I can think about is a man standing against a wall.

I pour myself a glass anyway and decide to take a warm bath to quiet my mind. I fill the tub and add the salts, letting the scent of lavender spread through the bathroom. Then I lower myself into the water and rest my head against the edge.

For a moment, I feel the warmth settle into my tired muscles.

I close my eyes.

And I'm back in that doorway.

The first thing I noticed was the sound: the rhythmic knock of a shoulder against the wall, steady as a heartbeat. I stopped with one hand still on the frame, the other forgotten at my side. They hadn’t heard me. 

He had her pinned against the wall, and the whole line of his body moved with a slow, but hard rhythm. Her skirt was shoved up around her waist. One of her legs was hooked around his hip, I could see the tension in it, the heel pressing into the back of his thigh each time he drove forward.

The shirt hung open on her. It had slipped off one shoulder, and his mouth was on her breast, his head bowed, sucking her nipple. Each time his mouth moved, her back arched off the wall to meet him. Her fingers were buried in his hair, then raking down his neck, then clutching at his shirt, restless and greedy, never staying in one place for long. She pulled him closer whenever he lifted his head, unwilling to let even a breath of distance settle between them.

There was nothing hesitant about them.

They moved as if the rest of the world didn't exist. As if there were no consequences waiting outside that room, no lives that might be hurt, no tomorrow to answer for.

From where I stood, I could see the dark lace pulled aside at the point where their bodies joined, the glistening, the shameless proof of it. I could hear everything: the wet, rough sound of his breath against her skin, the small noises she made each time he pushed deep, the faint slap of flesh. The air in the room seemed to have thickened around them, warm and close, tasting of sweat.

He lifted his head, his mouth found hers again and I caught his face in profile, eyes shut, mouth open, undone. It was the way he turned his head. The angle of his jaw caught in the light, and my mind, which had been so busy filling the scene, stopped mid-sentence. He said something low against her throat and the woman laughed, a shaky, breathless sound that turned into a moan as he pushed his hips and went deeper. 

I couldn’t make it out since it was too low for me to hear the words, but I knew the sound of it anyway. Seven years of silence, and my body still knew that voice before I did.

I knew that face. I knew that voice. I knew that man. 

I was holding the doorframe so tightly that my fingers began to ache.

The recognition did not hurt at first. It stilled me. Everything in my chest went quiet, the way a room goes quiet after something has fallen and no one has yet seen what broke. Then he laughed softly at something she said, and the silence inside me shattered.

It was him. The love of my life.

No.

No, no, no.

I wanted to leave. I wanted to run. And I wanted to stay.

I wanted him to look at me. And I wanted him not to.

Because what would I have done if he'd recognized me? What would I have said if our eyes had met and he'd known exactly who I was?

Would he have stopped? Would he have stepped away from her? Would he have said my name? Or would he have looked at me with the polite confusion reserved for strangers?

I couldn't bear to find out.

Finally, they saw me. I stumbled away before either of them could say anything. I mumbled an apology that made no sense and hurried toward the bathrooms, my heart pounding so violently I thought I might be sick. He couldn't possibly recognize me from my eyes alone since the uniform had a face mask. Could he?

The eyes… 

I squeeze mine shut for a second.

And now, sitting in my bathtub seven years later, or seven years too late, I don't know which, I finally understand why I couldn't move. It wasn't simply the shock of seeing him. It was the realization that I had never really let him go. 

The pain arrives slowly, spreading beneath my ribs, opening an old wound I had spent seven years teaching myself to live with.

It isn’t the pain of being betrayed. He had never promised me anything. He hadn't betrayed me. He hadn't broken a vow or walked out on a life we'd built together. That is the cruelty of it. He owed me nothing, and I have no claim to the ache spreading through my chest. I know that. I understand it perfectly. And it hurts anyway. It rises slowly from somewhere beneath my ribs, an old wound I had spent seven years teaching myself to walk on, pulled open with a single glance. I had told myself it had healed. I had told myself so often and so firmly that I had almost believed the scar was the skin.

I think of all the things we had never been. 

The apartment we had never rented, the mornings we had never shared, the ordinary arguments, the quiet dinners, the evenings spent doing absolutely nothing together. All the little things that make up a life, the years that had been laid out between us, like a road that I didn’t dare to take. We had loved each other with an intensity that was both our salvation and our undoing, and in the end it had curdled into silence because silence was easier than the alternative. No final argument. No slammed door. Just two people who stopped answering, one unanswered message at a time, until the quiet became permanent.

And then the years began.

I learned to live without hearing his voice. I stopped checking my phone for messages that weren't coming. I stopped looking for excuses to reach out to him. 

I built a life. Not the life I'd imagined, perhaps, but a life all the same. I moved away. I found work. I survived my illness, or learned to live alongside it. I worked until my body ached and my eyes blurred from exhaustion. I learned to count every expense, to make a home out of a small apartment, to find happiness in the smallest things. A clean bed. A warm bath. A bottle of wine on my birthday.

And I had kept a place for him. That was what I understood then, standing in the doorway with my hand locked around the frame. In some hidden room of myself I had kept the light on for seven years, never admitting it, never expecting him to walk in. Some foolish part of me had believed that if the world ever brought us back together, it would be to that room, to the unfinished conversation, to the love that had been waiting.

I had never imagined a wall. A stranger's laughter. His hands resting on someone else's body, doing what I had only ever allowed myself to imagine.

The intimacy of a man who had once known me so well that he could hear the difference between my ordinary silence and my unhappy one, now whispering into another woman's neck.

And I have no right to feel betrayed.

That is the worst part.

My breath catches and will not release. My chest feels hollowed out and overfull at once, as though grief and longing are fighting over the same small space. It isn’t jealousy, or not only jealousy. It is the terrible, quiet arithmetic of everything I have lost without ever having held it. I mourned him once already, years ago, and believed that mourning was finished. I didn’t know grief can be rehearsed and still arrive new.

My eyes burn. I do not let them spill. _You gave him up_, I remind myself. But the thought offers no comfort, only the cold, clear understanding that being right has never once made it hurt less.

I'm simply tired.

I'm tired of losing things. Tired of working until I have nothing left to give. Tired of pretending that the little life I've built is enough when there are still parts of me that remember wanting so much more.

I open my eyes. The bathroom is quiet. The water has begun to cool. 

I think of the person I was four months ago, putting aside a little money every week, imagining this evening. I think of how excited I was to come home, to lock the door, to have one small thing that belonged entirely to me.

I wonder what that person would say if they could see me now. Would they tell me to forget him? Would they remind me that he is not mine, that seven years is a long time, that people change and lives move on?

Of course they would.

I know all of that already. But there is a difference between understanding something and being able to live with it.

I lift my hand from the water and watch the droplets slide down my wrist.

Somewhere, in that office, he is probably still with her. Perhaps he has already forgotten the evening. Perhaps he never even noticed the cleaner standing in the doorway. Perhaps tomorrow he will wake up beside someone he desires, drink his coffee, and return to his life without giving me a second thought. And I will go back to work.

I will stock shelves. I will clean offices. I will count my money and pay my medical bills. I will keep the little apartment tidy and buy myself something nice whenever I can afford it.

I will swim. I've become very good at swimming against the current. But tonight, for the first time in years, I wish I knew how to move with the water, instead of always fighting its current.

I get out of the bath and wrap myself in a towel. The mirror is fogged, and I wipe a small circle in the glass with my palm.

Thirty-eight.

I look older than I feel. Or perhaps I feel older than I am.

I walk into the kitchen picking up my glass of wine. I take a sip and force myself to swallow.

Then I sit on the sofa, draw the blanket over my legs, and look at the book waiting on the table. I don't open it. I don't turn on the music. I sit in the quiet apartment I've worked so hard to make a home, and I let myself remember him. Not the man against the wall. Not the wedding ring, or the woman, or the laughter.

I remember his voice saying my name. I remember the way he used to look at me when I was talking about something that mattered to me. I remember the future we never had, and the people we might have become if I'd been braver.

I remember loving him.

And then, finally, I allow myself to grieve.

Because perhaps that is what I have been doing all along, without ever admitting it. Grieving a person who is still alive. Grieving a relationship that never quite became one. Grieving a future that existed only in the space between what I felt and what I dared to do about it.

My wine sits forgotten on the table. 

Outside, the city carries on. Somewhere, people are laughing, working, falling in love, breaking up, living the lives they were given or the lives they chose.

And somewhere in this same city, the man I never forgot is breathing the same air as me.

For seven years, I didn't know where he was.

Now I know exactly where he is.

And exactly how far away.
